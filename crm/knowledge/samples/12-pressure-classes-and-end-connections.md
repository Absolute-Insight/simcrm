## What it is

Two things must be right for a valve to fit and to be safe: its **pressure class** and its **end connection**. The class is the pressure–temperature rating the body carries; the end connection is how it bolts, screws or welds into the line. A valve that is the right size and class but drilled to the wrong flange table will not fit — and the wrong class fitted to matching flanges is a safety problem.

## Typical sizes and ratings

**ASME / ANSI classes** (Class 150, 300, 600, 900, 1500, 2500) are ratings, not pressures: the actual allowable pressure depends on the material group and temperature, tabulated in ASME B16.34 for valves and ASME B16.5 for flanges. As a rule of thumb for carbon steel at ambient: Class 150 ≈ 19–20 bar, Class 300 ≈ 50 bar, Class 600 ≈ 100 bar. The ratings fall with temperature — Class 150 is only about 5.5 bar at 400 °C.

**PN ratings** (PN 6, 10, 16, 25, 40, 63, 100) from the European and ISO systems are nominal pressures in bar at ambient, again derated with temperature. PN 16 is the common water-works rating.

**Nominal sizes** are DN (mm, nominal bore) or NPS (inches). DN 50 = NPS 2; DN 100 = NPS 4; DN 150 = NPS 6; DN 200 = NPS 8; DN 300 = NPS 12.

**Face-to-face** dimensions are standardised (ASME B16.10, EN 558 / ISO 5752) so a valve from one maker replaces another. Always check the series when replacing a valve in an existing gap.

## Materials

Not applicable as such — but note that a class is only valid for the material listed on the nameplate, and that cast iron valves have their own lower ratings and must never be used where a steel class was specified.

## Where it is used

Flange standards in South African practice:

- **SANS 1123 (ex SABS 1123)**: the local flange standard, with tables named by pressure: 600/3 (Table C), 1000/3 (Table D), 1600/3 (Table E), 2500/3 (Table F), 4000/3 (Table H), and Table T. The number is the pressure in kPa; "/3" is the flange type. Table D and Table E are the everyday water and mine-service drillings.
- **BS 10** (Tables D, E, F, H): the older British standard from which SANS 1123 descends; many drillings are interchangeable but check.
- **ASME B16.5** (Class 150, 300 and up): petrochemical, refining, power and any plant built to American practice; raised face is normal, RTJ for high pressure.
- **EN 1092-1 / ISO 7005** (PN 10, 16, 25, 40): European-supplied equipment, and increasingly water works.

SANS 1123 1600/3 (Table E) and EN 1092 PN 16 share the same drilling in many sizes, and Table D and PN 10 likewise — but not all sizes, so confirm from the tables rather than assuming.

Other ends: **threaded** (BSP parallel or taper to ISO 7 / ISO 228, NPT to ASME B1.20.1 — they do not mate), **socket weld** and **butt weld** (ASME B16.11 and B16.25, for high pressure and steam), **wafer** and **lug** (clamped between flanges), grooved (fire protection), and hygienic clamp fittings (food).

## Strengths and limits

Rated classes make valves interchangeable across suppliers, but only inside one system. Mixing a PN 16 valve into a Class 150 line, or a Table D valve into a Table E line, is the most common site fitting problem — and the mismatch is not always visible until the bolts do not line up.

## Questions to ask the customer

- What flange standard and table or class are the existing flanges? If they do not know: bolt count, PCD and bolt hole size settle it.
- Design pressure and temperature — not just operating — so the class can be checked.
- Raised face, flat face or RTJ? Cast iron flanges are flat face and must not be bolted to a raised face.
- Face-to-face dimension available if this is a replacement.
- Threaded ends: BSP or NPT?

## Standards
