## What it is

Valve and instrumentation buyers specify three different kinds of standard, and a good answer on a call keeps them apart:

1. **Product design standards** define how a valve is built, sized and dimensioned — API, ASME, BS / EN, ISO, JIS and SANS documents such as API 600 or ASME B16.34.
2. **Test and performance standards** define what a finished valve must pass — pressure testing (API 598, ISO 5208), fire testing (API 607, API 6FA, ISO 10497), fugitive emissions (ISO 15848, API 641).
3. **Management system certifications** say something about the supplier, not the product. The three that appear on every tender are:
   - **ISO 9001:2015** — a quality management system: the company has documented, audited processes for controlling how it designs, sources, makes, inspects and delivers, and for correcting what goes wrong.
   - **ISO 45001:2018** — an occupational health and safety management system: hazards to workers and site visitors are identified, controlled and reviewed, which is why mines and petrochemical clients ask for it before allowing contractors on site.
   - **ISO 14001:2015** — an environmental management system: the company measures and reduces its environmental impacts (waste, emissions, resource use) and complies with environmental law.

The **standards bodies** behind the letters: **ISO** (international), **ANSI** (the American national body; "ANSI class" is the everyday name for ASME B16 classes), **ASME** (American mechanical engineering codes — B16 flanges and valves, Section VIII vessels), **API** (American Petroleum Institute — oil and gas valve standards), **BS / EN** (British and harmonised European standards), **JIS** (Japanese Industrial Standards — JIS 10K and 20K flanges appear on Japanese-built equipment), and **SABS / SANS** (South African Bureau of Standards; standards are now published as SANS numbers, and the SABS mark is a product certification).

## Materials

Material traceability is documented under **EN 10204**: a **3.1** certificate is the maker's own inspection certificate for the actual material supplied; a **3.2** certificate is countersigned by an independent inspector. Petrochemical and power customers usually specify 3.1 for pressure-containing parts. Positive material identification (PMI) is a site check that the alloy is what the certificate says.

## Where it is used

- **Petrochemical and refining:** API design standards, API 598 testing, API 607 fire-safe, NACE MR0175 sour service, ISO 15848 fugitive emissions, 3.1 certificates.
- **Power generation and steam:** ASME codes, chrome-moly material certificates, hydrostatic test certificates.
- **Mining and minerals processing:** SANS flanges, ISO 45001 for site access, mine-specific standards on slurry valves.
- **Water and potable reticulation:** SANS 1123 flanges, SANS 664 / 1551 water valves, potable-water approvals for coatings and elastomers, SANS 241 water quality.
- **Fire protection:** listed and approved products (FM, UL, or SABS approval) are typically mandatory.
- **Food and beverage:** FDA / EC 1935 food-contact materials, hygienic design standards (EHEDG, 3-A).

## Strengths and limits

A management certification proves a system, not a valve — an ISO 9001 supplier can still ship the wrong product if the specification was wrong. Conversely, a product standard says nothing about delivery, documentation or support. Tenders usually need both, and a valve "to API 600" without a test certificate to API 598 is incomplete paperwork.

## Questions to ask the customer

- Which design standard and which test standard does the specification call for?
- Are material certificates (3.1 or 3.2) required, and for which parts?
- Fire-safe, fugitive-emissions or sour-service requirements?
- Does the site require ISO 45001 or a specific safety file for delivery and installation?
- Is the equipment under the Pressure Equipment Regulations (SANS 347 categorisation)?
