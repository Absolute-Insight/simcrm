## What it is

An actuator operates a valve remotely or automatically. It is chosen by the valve's motion (quarter-turn for ball, butterfly and plug; multi-turn for gate and globe; linear for control valves), by the torque the valve needs, and by the power on site.

- **Pneumatic** actuators run on instrument air at 4–7 bar. **Rack and pinion** units are compact and cheap for small quarter-turn valves; **scotch yoke** units give high torque for large ones. **Double-acting** units need air both ways; **spring-return** units fail to a known position on loss of air. A **solenoid valve** (often NAMUR-mounted) switches the air, and a **switch box** with limit switches or a position transmitter reports open and closed to the control system.
- **Electric** actuators use a motor and gear train with torque and position limits, local controls and a handwheel override. On–off, modulating (4–20 mA) and fieldbus versions exist; fail-safe needs a spring or battery pack.
- **Hydraulic** and electro-hydraulic actuators give very high force in a small envelope for large or high-pressure valves.
- **Gearboxes** (bevel for gate and globe, worm for quarter-turn) reduce manual effort and mount an actuator to a large valve; declutchable gearboxes allow manual override.

## Typical sizes and ratings

- Quarter-turn pneumatic: about 5 Nm to 100,000 Nm; electric multi-turn to tens of thousands of Nm.
- Mounting to ISO 5211 flanges (F03 to F60), so valve and actuator from different suppliers fit.
- Supplies of 24 V DC, 230 V or 400 V three-phase; enclosures IP67 / IP68; Ex d for hazardous areas.

## Materials

Pneumatic bodies in anodised or coated aluminium; stainless or nickel-plated for corrosive and food plants. Electric housings in epoxy-coated aluminium. Gearbox bodies in ductile iron.

## Where it is used

- **Water and wastewater:** electric actuators on large gate and butterfly valves with SCADA feedback.
- **Mining, minerals processing and slurry:** pneumatic cylinders and heavy-duty electrics on knife gates; dust and wash-down enclosures.
- **Petrochemical, refining, power generation and steam:** spring-return pneumatics on emergency shutdown valves; Ex-rated and modulating electrics on tank farms, feedwater and drains.
- **Food and beverage, pulp and paper, fire protection:** stainless rack-and-pinion with switch boxes on process and CIP valves; supervised switch boxes on sprinkler isolation.

## Strengths and limits

Pneumatic: simple, cheap, inherently fail-safe, safe in hazardous areas — but needs clean dry air and a positioner for position control. Electric: precise, self-contained, easy to integrate — but slower, and fail-safe costs extra. Hydraulic: highest force — but needs a power pack. Size on the valve's break-to-open torque with a 25–30% safety factor at the minimum supply pressure.

## Questions to ask the customer

- Which valve — type, size, class, torque figure? If unknown, make and model.
- What power is at the valve: instrument air (at what pressure), 24 V DC, 230 V, 400 V?
- Fail position on loss of power or air? On–off or modulating, and what feedback?
- Hazardous area? Outdoor, wash-down or corrosive environment?

## Standards

ISO 5211 (part-turn attachment), ISO 5210 (multi-turn attachment), EN 15714 (industrial valve actuators), NAMUR VDI/VDE 3845 (solenoid and switch box interface), IEC 60529 (IP ratings), IEC 60079 / ATEX / IECEx (hazardous areas), IEC 61508 (SIL-rated fail-safe actuators).
